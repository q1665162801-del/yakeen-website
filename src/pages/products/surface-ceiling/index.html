Product Family

Six surface families, one clean junction.

A surface luminaire is judged on two things a recessed fixture can hide: the junction with the ceiling, and the heat path out of a body that has no ceiling void to dissipate into. Both are addressed in the housing.

How to choose

Three questions worth asking early.

Surface lighting is usually specified late, once the ceiling build-up is fixed. Ask these before the ceiling detail is drawn.

01

Is there a ceiling void?

No void → surface cylinder or drum, not a recessed downlight. Trying to recess into a 60mm slab service zone is how ceilings get cut open after handover. If the void exists, the Downlights line is the better answer.

02

How high is the mounting plane?

Below 3m → standard cylinder with UGR<19. Between 3 and 6m → higher output and narrower optics. Above 6m → high-ceiling or suspended, where access for maintenance also becomes a design decision.

03

Is it a circulation or service area?

Car parks, service corridors, plant rooms and back-of-house need IP65 and IK10 — and usually an emergency variant on the same body so the ceiling does not carry two different fixture types.

Engineering Deep Dive

No void, no free heatsink. So the body becomes one.

Surface fixtures fail earlier than recessed ones for a simple reason: all the heat leaves through the visible body. In a 50°C plant room or an unventilated soffit, that changes the thermal design completely.

01

The base is the heatsink

A recessed downlight dumps heat into the void. A surface cylinder has only its contact patch with the slab and its own surface area. We size the base casting accordingly — which is why a 20W surface cylinder is physically larger than a 20W recessed downlight. That is a thermal requirement, not styling.

02

Driver placement decides the profile

An integral driver inside the body adds its own heat to the LED chamber. Where the ceiling detail is tight we move the driver remote into the ceiling void or a nearby accessible enclosure, and the body shrinks. Both options are drawn, so the architect can see the consequence before approving the profile.

03

Mounting height changes the optic

The same 36° beam covers a 2m pool of light at 3m and a 7.6m pool at 12m — but the intensity per square metre drops with the square of the distance. High-ceiling bodies therefore pair higher output with narrower optics, so the beam reaches the task plane instead of washing the racking.

The result: a published thermal rating for the surface condition, a documented remote-driver option for tight soffits, and a beam table by mounting height. Ask for the thermal report with the datasheet.
