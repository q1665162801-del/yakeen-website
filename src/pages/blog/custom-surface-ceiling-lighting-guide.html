Custom Surface and Ceiling Lighting When There Is No Void

A shallow slab, a concrete soffit or a finished ceiling with no cavity changes the whole approach. Here is how surface, suspended and pendant solutions are detailed.

When the void disappears

Recessed lighting assumes a cavity. On many projects there is not one. Exposed concrete soffits, slabs without a suspended ceiling, refurbishments where the original void has been filled with services, and value-engineered ceilings with 50mm of clearance all produce the same constraint: the fixture has to live below the surface.

Design teams often treat this as a downgrade. It is not. Surface-mounted and suspended lighting is a legitimate architectural language, and in the right interior it reads as more deliberate than a ceiling full of holes. The failure mode is not surface lighting itself; it is treating surface lighting as a fallback and applying it without a composition.

What changes is the discipline of the layout. A recessed scheme can be forgiving because the eye reads the aperture, not the body. A surface scheme is read as objects in the room, so size, spacing, alignment and color all become visible design decisions.

Surface fixtures as architecture

There are five families worth knowing, and they solve different problems:

  • Surface cylinders and downlights. A compact body fixed directly to the ceiling. The simplest route where the void is shallow and the ceiling is already finished. Size selection matters: a 90mm body reads as a detail; a 200mm body reads as a drum.
  • Surface linear profiles. Straight runs fixed to the soffit. Useful for circulation, corridors and retail perimeters where a continuous line is wanted and there is no slot to recess into.
  • Bulkhead and drum fittings. Larger bodies for high ceilings, service areas and façades. Robust, and honest about their presence.
  • Square and rectangular panels. A grid-friendly option for offices and back-of-house, where alignment with the ceiling module matters more than subtlety.
  • Pendant and suspended systems. Where the ceiling is too high, too busy or too exposed for direct fixing, the fixture comes down to the working plane on rods or cables.

On a bare concrete soffit, the honest approach is usually to celebrate the surface: a consistent spacing grid, a single body size, a finish that either matches the concrete or contrasts with it deliberately. Mixing three body types across one soffit produces a ceiling that looks assembled from leftovers.

The shallow-body problem

Where a small recess is available — 40mm to 60mm, typically in a plasterboard ceiling over a shallow service zone — the specification moves to a shallow body rather than a fully surface-mounted one. These exist, but the trade-offs need to be stated rather than discovered.

A shallow body means a smaller heat sink and a shorter optical path. In practice that usually means lower output per aperture, a wider minimum beam angle, and a driver that has to be remote or integrated into a very small space. Deep recess and glare control are compromised, because there is no depth to hide the source.

The practical answer is often a hybrid: shallow recessed fittings in the general field where the void allows, surface fittings on the beams and bulkheads where it does not, and a deliberate visual relationship between the two. Trying to make one body do both jobs usually produces a compromise in both.

Suspension and pendant solutions

Suspension solves three problems at once: it removes the need for a void, it brings light closer to the working plane, and it gives the designer control over the vertical composition of the room. On exposed soffits and double-height spaces it is usually the only sensible option.

  • Rod suspension. Rigid, precise, and the default for linear systems and larger pendants. The rod centers become part of the visual rhythm, so they need to be set out on a module.
  • Cable suspension. Lighter and less obtrusive, with more freedom in plan. Better for small pendants and for ceilings where the fixing points are irregular.
  • Combined power and suspension. A single drop that carries both structure and supply. Cleaner visually, and it removes the separate cable droppers that otherwise have to be dressed.
  • Track or trunking systems. Where fittings must be added or moved later, a suspended track carries both the mechanical and the electrical infrastructure.

Suspension length is a design decision, not a default. A pendant hung 300mm below a 6m soffit disappears; the same pendant hung 2.5m below it defines the space. Set the mounting height against the activities below before choosing the drop length, and confirm the ceiling anchor type at the same time.

Fixing, weight and structure

Everything that hangs or sits on a soffit is a structural question, and this is where surface schemes fail in practice. A concrete soffit will take a drilled anchor. A plasterboard ceiling with a 400mm void will not take a 12kg pendant without a dedicated support, even if the board is double-layered.

Three things need to be confirmed before the order is placed: the substrate, the anchor type, and who supplies the anchors. On new-build projects the support is usually cast in or fixed to the slab by the main contractor. On refurbishment, the support is often improvised, which is exactly when a fitting comes down.

Weight also accumulates along a run. A 6m suspended linear system with an integral driver is heavier than the sum of its parts suggests, and the rod spacing has to suit the load. Ask for the weight per meter and the maximum rod spacing as separate numbers, and check them against the fixing positions you actually have.

Cable, driver and access

With no void, there is nowhere to hide anything. Cable routes, drivers, connectors and dimming modules all have to be resolved on the visible side of the ceiling, and the detailing has to make that look intentional.

For surface fixtures, the usual solutions are a shallow surface conduit, a trunking system, or a fitting designed with an integral driver and a rear cable entry that can be dressed neatly. For pendants, the supply can run inside the suspension. For track, the conductors are inside the profile.

Drivers are the recurring problem. An integral driver simplifies the ceiling but puts heat next to the LED and makes replacement a fixture-level operation. A remote driver needs a serviceable position, which on a void-free ceiling means a cupboard, a riser or a dedicated enclosure. Decide which trade-off you are accepting, and write it into the specification.

Dimming control adds one more box to place. On a visible ceiling, that box becomes part of the design whether anyone planned it or not.

Documenting the requirement

Send the ceiling section or a photograph of the soffit, the available depth if any, the substrate and its thickness, the ceiling height, the layout intent, the required output and color quality, and the control protocol. Where the scheme is suspended, mark the fixing grid on the plan.
