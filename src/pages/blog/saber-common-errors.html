SABER Registration: 10 Common Errors and How to Fix Them

How SABER registration works for lighting

The SABER platform (منصة سابر) is the Saudi electronic system for product conformity assessment, operated by SASO (الهيئة السعودية للمواصفات والقياس والجودة). For lighting products entering Saudi Arabia (المملكة العربية السعودية), SABER is the mandatory registration pathway — there is no alternative.

The process has two key stages:

  • Product Certificate of Conformity (PCoC) — Issued for each product model, valid for one year. Based on test reports and SASO IECEE recognition. This is the "product is approved" step.
  • Shipment Certificate of Conformity (SCoC) — Issued for each shipment, valid for 60 days. Based on the PCoC plus shipment details. This is the "this shipment is approved" step.

Most problems occur during PCoC application. Once you have a valid PCoC, the SCoC is usually straightforward — unless you make one of the common shipment errors. Based on our team's experience with lighting product registrations for projects across Riyadh (الرياض), Jeddah (جدة), and Dammam (الدمام), these are the 10 errors we see most frequently.

1. Product model mismatch on PCoC

This is the single most common SABER rejection reason. The model number on the test report, the model number on the SASO certificate, and the model number you type into SABER do not match exactly. Not "close enough" — exactly.

Why it happens

Suppliers often use slightly different model naming conventions in different contexts. The factory might call it "DL-20W-30K", the datasheet says "DL20W3000K", and the test report says "DL-20-3000". Each version is understandable to a human, but SABER's system does exact string matching. Any discrepancy triggers an automatic rejection.

How to fix it

You have two options: (1) get the test report and certificate amended to match the exact model name you want to use in SABER, or (2) change the model name in SABER to match exactly what is on the certificate. Option 2 is almost always faster. Amending a test report at the lab can take 2–4 weeks. Changing the model name in the SABER application takes 5 minutes.

Prevention

Before you start the SABER application, copy the exact model number from the SASO IECEE Recognition Certificate and paste it into the SABER form. Do not retype it. Do not "standardize" it. Use exactly what is on the certificate, character for character.

2. Test report not from accredited lab

SASO only accepts test reports from laboratories accredited under the IECEE CB scheme. Reports from labs that are ISO 17025 accredited but not IECEE CB accredited will be rejected.

Why it happens

Many manufacturers have test reports from local labs that are cheaper and faster than IECEE-accredited labs. The reports look professional. The lab might even say "ISO 17025 accredited" — which is true, but insufficient. SASO specifically requires IECEE CB recognition for the standards being applied.

How to fix it

If you only have non-IECEE test reports, you need to retest at an accredited laboratory. There is no workaround. Some labs offer a "CB conversion" service where they review your existing test data and issue a CB report after supplementary testing if needed. This can save time and cost compared to a full retest, but the lab must be IECEE-accredited for the relevant standards.

Prevention

When sourcing products, explicitly ask: "Is this test report from an IECEE CB-accredited laboratory?" Then verify the lab's accreditation on the IECEE website. Do not accept "the lab is very reputable" or "they do work for other Saudi clients" — check the actual accreditation scope.

3. Missing Saudi national differences

A CB test report covers the base IEC standard. But SASO has national differences — specific requirements that go beyond IEC — and your CB report must demonstrate compliance with those Saudi national deviations.

Why it happens

Manufacturers often obtain CB reports for the "generic" IEC standard without testing for the specific national differences of Saudi Arabia. They then assume the CB report is sufficient for any CB scheme member country. It is not.

Key Saudi national differences for lighting products include:

  • Voltage and frequency: 230V / 60Hz (not 220V/50Hz or 240V/50Hz)
  • Plug type: Type G (BS 1363) for most applications
  • Arabic language requirements for marking and instructions
  • Specific energy efficiency thresholds set by SEEC

How to fix it

You need to supplement the CB report with additional testing or documentation covering the Saudi national differences. This is called a "national differences addendum" or "Saudi deviation report." The issuing lab can usually produce this if they tested with the Saudi deviations in mind. If not, you may need additional testing.

Prevention

When commissioning testing, explicitly tell the lab you need a CB report with Saudi Arabian national differences tested. Give them the SASO reference. A few extra days of testing upfront avoids weeks of delay later.

4. Arabic marking non-compliant

SASO 2902 requires product marking in Arabic (or Arabic + English). Products with English-only marking will fail SABER registration and customs inspection. But it is not just "add some Arabic" — the Arabic has to be correct and complete.

Why it happens

Common Arabic marking issues we have seen:

  • No Arabic at all — English-only labels
  • Arabic present but only for some information (e.g., brand in Arabic, but technical specs in English only)
  • Incorrect Arabic — Google Translate quality, with wrong technical terms
  • Arabic on the packaging but not on the product itself
  • Arabic text too small to be legible
  • Arabic printed on a sticker that peels off easily

How to fix it

If the product has already been manufactured without Arabic marking, you have a problem. Applying new labels in the factory is straightforward — re-labeling in a port warehouse is possible but expensive and time-consuming. For the SABER application itself, you can sometimes submit photos of corrected marking as evidence, but SABER may request a physical inspection at their discretion.

Prevention

Get your Arabic label text reviewed by a native Arabic speaker with technical knowledge of lighting products. The standard technical terms in Arabic are specific — "لومن" for lumens, "واط" for watts, "جهد مقدر" for rated voltage — and getting them wrong is a common reason for rejection. Also verify that the Arabic is on the product itself, not just the carton.

5. Energy efficiency registration missing

Many applicants focus on the safety certification (SASO IECEE Recognition) and forget that energy efficiency registration is a separate, mandatory requirement for lighting products under SASO 2902.

Why it happens

Safety certification and energy efficiency registration are two separate processes with two different Saudi authorities. Safety is handled by SASO directly. Energy efficiency is handled by the Saudi Energy Efficiency Center — SEEC (المركز السعودي لكفاءة الطاقة, formerly SEEC), which collaborates with SASO but has its own registration system and database.

It is common for a product to have valid SASO IECEE safety recognition but no SEEC energy efficiency registration. The SABER system checks both — if the energy efficiency registration is missing, the PCoC application will be rejected.

How to fix it

You need to register the product in the SEEC energy efficiency database. This requires submitting the photometric test report showing efficacy, power factor, and standby power data. SEEC reviews the application and assigns an efficiency class. The review typically takes 2–4 weeks. Once registered, you can resume the SABER PCoC application.

Prevention

Start the SEEC energy efficiency registration at the same time you start the SASO IECEE safety recognition process. Do not wait for safety to be approved first — run them in parallel. Both are required for SABER registration, and there is no reason to do them sequentially.

6. Certificate holder vs. importer mismatch

The company that holds the SASO certification and the company applying for SABER registration must be the same, or there must be a formal authorization link between them.

Why it happens

Scenario: A lighting manufacturer in China has SASO certification in their name. A Saudi trading company wants to import the product under their own SABER account. The trading company applies for PCoC using the manufacturer's certificate. SABER rejects it because the certificate holder and the applicant are different legal entities.

This also happens with private label arrangements: the product is made by Factory A but branded for Company B. If the certificate is in Factory A's name, Company B cannot use it directly.

How to fix it

There are two legitimate solutions:

  • Authorization letter: The certificate holder issues a formal authorization letter allowing the importer to use their certificate for SABER registration. The letter must be on company letterhead, signed by an authorized signatory, and clearly state which products and which importer are covered.
  • Certificate transfer: The certificate is formally transferred from the original holder to the new holder. This requires an application to SASO and typically takes 2–4 weeks.

Prevention

Before placing an order, confirm who holds the SASO certification and whether they will authorize you to use it. Get the authorization letter in advance, not when the shipment is already on the water.

7. HS code classification error

SABER requires you to specify the HS code for each product. If you select the wrong HS code, the system applies the wrong regulatory requirements, which can lead to either rejection (if you claim a code with looser requirements) or unnecessary testing costs (if you select a code with stricter requirements than needed).

Why it happens

Lighting products span multiple HS code headings — 9405 for lamps and lighting fixtures, 8539 for filament lamps and discharge lamps, 8504 for LED drivers, 8512 for vehicle lighting, and more. Within each heading there are multiple subheadings. Classifying a complex product like an LED luminaire with integrated driver and control gear requires understanding both the product and the tariff structure.

For lighting products, the most common misclassification is putting everything under 9405.11 / 9405.42 (HS2022 — verify current HTS classification before filing) when some products should be under different subheadings or even different chapters.

How to fix it

If you realize the HS code is wrong before submitting, just correct it. If SABER has already rejected your application because of incorrect classification, you need to reapply with the correct code. If customs has already flagged the shipment, you may need to provide a detailed classification rationale to the Saudi Customs Authority — الجمارك السعودية.

Prevention

Work with an experienced customs broker or trade consultant who specializes in lighting products for the Saudi market. A few hundred dollars in professional classification advice can save thousands in customs penalties and delays.

8. Shipment CoC rejected at customs

You have a valid PCoC. You apply for the Shipment CoC (SCoC). It gets rejected. This is frustrating because you thought the hard part was over.

Why it happens

Common SCoC rejection reasons:

  • Quantity mismatch: The shipment quantity exceeds what is authorized on the PCoC, or the unit of measure is wrong.
  • Value discrepancy: The declared value on the commercial invoice is significantly different from the value used in the PCoC application. Large gaps trigger customs valuation scrutiny.
  • New model in shipment: You added a product model to the shipment that was not on the original PCoC.
  • PCoC expired: The PCoC expired between when you applied for it and when the shipment arrives. PCoCs are valid for one year from issuance.

How to fix it

For quantity or value discrepancies, you may need to provide additional documentation justifying the difference. For an unlisted model, you need to add that model to the PCoC first (which requires a PCoC amendment, typically 3–5 business days). For an expired PCoC, you need to renew it before you can get an SCoC.

Prevention

Before applying for an SCoC, double-check that every model in the shipment is on the PCoC, that the quantities are within the authorized range, and that the PCoC will still be valid on the expected arrival date. If the PCoC is expiring soon, renew it proactively — do not wait for it to expire.

9. Expired or near-expired certificates

SASO IECEE Recognition Certificates have a validity period (typically 3–5 years from issuance, depending on the product category). SEEC energy efficiency registrations also expire. SABER will reject applications based on expired certificates.

Why it happens

Suppliers often continue selling products with expired certificates because renewal is "in process" or "just paperwork." The reality is that certificate renewal involves a factory audit and/or retesting, and it can take months. An expired certificate is not valid for SABER registration, and there is no grace period.

This is especially common with product lines that sell steadily — the supplier got the certificate when the product launched, and three years later they are still selling the same product but the certificate has lapsed.

How to fix it

The supplier needs to renew the certificate with SASO. There is no shortcut. If you are in a hurry, ask the supplier if they have a pending renewal application and whether SASO can issue a temporary extension. Extensions are rare but possible in some circumstances.

Prevention

Always check the expiry date on every certificate before placing an order. If the certificate expires within 6 months of your planned shipment date, ask the supplier to confirm renewal status and timeline. If renewal is not complete before you need to ship, find an alternative product with current certification.

10. Family product overreach

The supplier has one model certified and claims that all similar models in the product family are covered. They are not. SABER registration is model-specific.

Why it happens

Lighting product families often have many variants — different wattages, color temperatures, beam angles, dimming options, mounting types. Testing every single variant is expensive, so manufacturers test one "representative" variant and assume the rest are similar enough. SASO's rules for what constitutes a valid "family grouping" are specific and narrower than most manufacturers assume.

The rules generally allow grouping when the differences are cosmetic or when the variants share the same critical components (driver, LED module, thermal design). But changes in wattage, optics, or driver type usually require separate testing and certification.

How to fix it

If SABER rejects a model because it is not covered by the existing certificate, you need to either: (1) get the specific variant certified (which requires testing and a new certificate), or (2) order only the variants that are actually listed on the existing certificate.

Prevention

Read the certificate carefully. Look for the list of covered models. If the variant you want is not explicitly listed, it is not covered. Ask the supplier for a copy of the test report and check the scope — does it say "model DL-20W only" or does it list all the variants? If in doubt, confirm with SASO or with a certification consultant before you order.

What to do when SABER rejects you

First, do not panic. SABER rejections are common, and most are fixable. Here is what to do:

Step 1: Read the rejection reason carefully

SABER will provide a rejection reason. It might be in Arabic, so get it translated if needed. Understand exactly what the problem is. Is it a document issue? A test report issue? A model mismatch? The solution depends entirely on the specific rejection reason.

Step 2: Assess the timeline

How urgent is this? If the shipment is already at sea and due in Jeddah Islamic Port next week, you have a different problem than if you are still in the pre-shipment planning phase. Be realistic about how long the fix will take:

  • Simple document corrections (model name change, missing document upload): 1–3 business days
  • Authorization letter or certificate holder issue: 1–2 weeks
  • Energy efficiency registration: 2–4 weeks
  • Missing national differences testing: 3–6 weeks
  • Full retest required: 6–10 weeks

Step 3: Communicate with your supplier

The supplier is usually responsible for providing compliant certification. Put them on notice immediately. Ask them what they are doing to fix the issue, and get a written timeline. If they are unresponsive or unable to fix it, you may need to engage a third-party certification consultant to help.

Step 4: Consider alternative clearance routes

If the shipment is already in port and the fix will take weeks, you have limited options:

  • Demand warehousing: You can move the shipment to a bonded warehouse while you resolve the issue, but you are paying storage fees every day.
  • Re-export: If the issue cannot be resolved, you may need to re-export the goods. This is expensive and disruptive.
  • Expedited certification: Some labs and consultants offer expedited services for an additional fee. Worth exploring if the cost of delay is high.

The best approach is to not let it get to this point. Pre-verify everything before the product ships.

Prevention: the pre-submission checklist

Before you submit a SABER PCoC application for any lighting product, run through this list:

SABER pre-submission checklist for lighting products

  • SASO IECEE Recognition Certificate is current and not expired
  • Exact model number matches certificate character-for-character
  • Test report from IECEE CB-accredited laboratory
  • Saudi national differences tested and documented
  • SEEC energy efficiency registration obtained
  • Certificate holder matches SABER applicant or authorization letter in place
  • Arabic product marking present and technically accurate
  • HS code correctly classified for the product type
  • All variants ordered are listed on the certificate
  • Voltage and frequency rating: 230V / 60Hz

SABER registration does not have to be painful. The problems almost always come from assuming compliance rather than verifying it. Spend a few hours doing due diligence on the front end, and the actual SABER application becomes a straightforward administrative task.
