12 Lighting Specification Mistakes That Cost You Money

Why specification mistakes are so expensive

Every lighting specification has mistakes. Some are minor and get caught during submittal review. Others make it all the way to installation, where they become expensive problems that eat into project budgets and schedules.

The cost of fixing a mistake multiplies at each stage of the project. A minor error in the specification becomes a procurement problem, then a site problem, and finally a post-handover problem that is orders of magnitude more painful to resolve. The cheapest place to fix a mistake is on the drawing board.

Drawing on our team's 10+ years reviewing lighting specifications and fixing problems on GCC projects, we have identified the 12 most common and most costly specification mistakes. Each one includes what goes wrong, why it happens, and how to avoid it.

Mistake 1: Underestimating glare (UGR)

The problem

Glare is the #1 complaint about lighting in occupied spaces. People may not know the word "UGR," but they know when a room feels uncomfortable to be in. Excessive glare causes eye strain, headaches, and a general sense that the space feels cheap — even if everything else is high quality.

Why it happens

Glare is often treated as an afterthought rather than a primary specification parameter. Many specifiers select fixtures based on output and wattage without checking the UGR value. Or they rely on UGR numbers from datasheets that are measured under ideal conditions, not real-world installation conditions.

How to avoid it:

  • Specify UGR values explicitly in your fixture schedule, not just output and wattage.
  • For general office and circulation areas, specify UGR ≤ 19. For areas where people sit for long periods (offices, hotel rooms, restaurants), aim for UGR ≤ 16.
  • Remember that UGR depends on mounting height, room dimensions, and reflectance. A fixture with UGR 19 in a high-ceilinged room might be UGR 22 in a low-ceilinged room.
  • Use DIALux or similar software to calculate actual UGR for the space, not just the fixture's rated UGR.
  • Pay special attention to seated eye height. In spaces where people sit (offices, restaurants, mosques), glare is more noticeable from a lower vantage point.
  • Specify deep-recessed LEDs, louvers, and reflectors for areas where glare control matters.

Mistake 2: Skimping on color rendering (CRI)

The problem

Low CRI lighting makes everything look worse. Materials look dull, food looks unappetizing, people look washed out. It erodes the perceived quality of the entire space. And once the fixtures are installed, there is no easy fix — you cannot upgrade the CRI of an LED after the fact.

Why it happens

CRI 80 fixtures are cheaper than CRI 90+ fixtures, and during the tender phase, the cost difference looks significant. But the actual premium for better color quality is small compared to the impact on the space.

How to avoid it:

  • Specify CRI 90+ as standard for all occupied spaces. CRI 95+ for areas where color quality is critical (retail, restaurants, art galleries, fitting rooms).
  • Pay attention to R9 (red rendering). A high CRI number does not guarantee good R9, and R9 is critical for skin tones, food, and red materials. Specify R9 > 50 minimum.
  • Do not use CRI 80 anywhere that people see or interact with each other. CRI 80 is acceptable for utility spaces, corridors, and storage — and that is about it.
  • Specify 3-step MacAdam ellipse color consistency. 5-step or 7-step fixtures will show visible color variation between units, especially when mounted in rows.
  • Always request a physical sample before approving fixtures. Photometric reports tell you the numbers, but you need to see the actual light quality.

Mistake 3: Wrong beam angles

The problem

Wrong beam angles are the most common reason lighting designs do not deliver on the intended effect. Too narrow and you get harsh pools of light with dark gaps in between. Too wide and everything is washed out, flat, and without contrast. The space feels wrong, and it is very difficult to fix on site.

Why it happens

Beam angle selection is often done by intuition or habit rather than calculation. A designer specifies "25° spot" because that is what they always use, without checking whether it works for the actual ceiling height and target size in this specific project.

How to avoid it:

  • Always calculate beam diameter at the target plane. Beam angle in degrees means nothing until you know the distance to the target.
  • Use the formula: beam diameter = 2 × distance × tan(beam angle / 2). Or use a beam angle calculator.
  • Consider what you are lighting. A small jewelry display needs a narrow beam; a large wall of shelving needs a wide wall wash.
  • For uniform area lighting, space fixtures so that the beam patterns overlap by 30–50%. Edge-to-edge spacing creates dark gaps.
  • For accent lighting, the beam should frame the target with a little overlap, not completely flood it or barely cover it.
  • Specify fixtures with interchangeable optics where possible. This gives you flexibility on site if the layout changes or the ceiling height is different than expected.

Mistake 4: Dimming incompatibility

The problem

You specify dimmable fixtures and a dimming control system, but on site, they do not work together properly. The lights flicker, they pop on at the bottom of the dimming range, there is a dead band at the top of the dimmer, or the dimming is not smooth. The lighting feels cheap, even with expensive fixtures.

Why it happens

Not all dimmable LEDs are compatible with all dimming systems. 0–10V, DALI, Triac, and DMX are all different. Even within the same protocol, some combinations work better than others. The problem is usually discovered during commissioning, when it is expensive and time-consuming to fix.

How to avoid it:

  • Specify the dimming protocol explicitly. Do not just write "dimmable" — write "DALI dimmable" or "0–10V dimmable."
  • Choose the control system first, then select fixtures that are certified compatible with that system.
  • Always request a dimming test with sample fixtures and the actual control system before finalizing the specification. 15 minutes of testing can save weeks of commissioning headaches.
  • Be specific about dimming performance requirements. Specify minimum dimming level (e.g., "dims smoothly to 1% without flicker"), dimming curve, and any flicker requirements.
  • Avoid mixing dimming protocols in the same space. It creates compatibility issues and makes commissioning more difficult.
  • For projects with multiple fixture types from different manufacturers, require a compatibility test before production.

Mistake 5: Ignoring thermal management

The problem

LEDs fail from heat, not from use. A poorly cooled LED will degrade rapidly, losing brightness and shifting color in just a few years. In the GCC climate, where ambient temperatures can reach 50°C or more, thermal management is not optional — it is the primary determinant of fixture lifespan.

Why it happens

Thermal performance is not visible in a render or a datasheet photo. It is easy to look at two downlights that look identical on the outside and assume they perform the same. But the difference is in the heat sink design, the LED board, and the driver quality — all things you cannot see.

How to avoid it:

  • Specify fixtures with proper heat sinks, not just decorative housings. The weight of the fixture is a rough indicator — more metal means better heat dissipation.
  • Check the rated ambient temperature (Ta) on the datasheet. For interior use in the GCC, look for Ta 45°C minimum. For exterior, Ta 55°C or higher.
  • Look for L70 or L80 lifespan ratings at the rated ambient temperature. L70 = 70% lumen maintenance at end of rated life.
  • Be skeptical of very high-output fixtures in small housings. If a 30W downlight is the same size as a 15W downlight, the 30W one is probably running hot and will not last.
  • For exterior and high-temperature environments, consider derating the fixture — running it at lower than maximum power to reduce heat and extend life.
  • Choose fixtures from manufacturers with a proven track record in hot climates. A fixture that works well in Germany may fail quickly in Dubai.

Mistake 6: Wrong IP rating

The problem

Fixtures installed in wet or dusty environments with insufficient IP rating fail prematurely. Water gets in, sand gets in, the electronics corrode, and the fixture stops working. In the GCC, where sandstorms and high humidity are common, this is one of the most frequent failure modes for exterior fixtures.

Why it happens

IP ratings are often specified generically ("IP65 for exterior") without considering the specific conditions. Not all IP65 fixtures are created equal — some have proper sealing and pressure equalization, others just have a rubber gasket that will degrade in a few years.

How to avoid it:

  • Understand what IP ratings actually mean. IP65 = dust-tight + water jets. IP66 = dust-tight + powerful water jets. IP67 = dust-tight + temporary immersion. IP68 = dust-tight + continuous immersion.
  • For general exterior use in the GCC, specify IP65 minimum. For ground-recessed fixtures (inground uplights), specify IP67 minimum because they can be submerged during heavy rain or cleaning.
  • For underwater applications, specify IP68 and verify the depth rating.
  • For bathrooms and wet areas, follow local electrical codes for zone-based IP requirements.
  • Look beyond the IP number. Quality of sealing, material selection, and pressure equalization valves are more important for long-term reliability than the IP rating alone.
  • Consider salt spray resistance for coastal locations. Standard aluminum fixtures will corrode in coastal environments — specify marine-grade aluminum or stainless steel.

Mistake 7: Insufficient installation space

The problem

The lighting design looks great on plan, but when it comes to installation, the fixtures do not fit in the ceiling. There is not enough depth, the joists are in the way, or there is a conflict with HVAC ducts, sprinklers, or other services. The result: last-minute fixture substitutions that compromise the design, or costly ceiling modifications.

Why it happens

Lighting is often designed in isolation from other building services. The lighting designer places fixtures on the reflected ceiling plan without coordinating with the MEP engineer. By the time the conflict is discovered, the ceiling design is already set and changing it is expensive.

How to avoid it:

  • Coordinate fixture locations with all other ceiling services early in the design process. HVAC, sprinklers, speakers, and smoke detectors all compete for ceiling space.
  • Check fixture cutout dimensions and depth requirements carefully. A fixture that looks shallow in plan might need a deep housing that conflicts with structure or ductwork.
  • For shallow ceiling plenum spaces, specify shallow-profile or surface-mount fixtures instead of deep recessed ones.
  • Use BIM (Revit or similar) to coordinate services in 3D. Clash detection catches most issues before construction.
  • Do not assume standard ceiling depths. Always confirm the available plenum depth with the project architect or structural engineer.
  • For retrofit projects, always survey existing ceiling conditions before finalizing the specification. Do not trust as-built drawings.

Mistake 8: Forgetting about maintenance

The problem

The lighting looks great on opening day, but two years later, a quarter of the fixtures are not working, and nobody can get to them to replace them. Or the replacement parts are no longer available. Or the cost of maintenance is so high that the owner just lives with broken fixtures.

Why it happens

Maintenance is almost always an afterthought in lighting design. The focus is on how it looks when it is new, not how it will be kept working for the next 10–20 years. And maintenance access is often not considered until after the building is finished.

How to avoid it:

  • Consider how each fixture will be replaced or serviced. If it is in a 10m high ceiling, how will someone reach it? Scaffolding? Cherry picker? Access panel?
  • For high-ceilinged and hard-to-access areas, specify longer-life fixtures. The premium for a 75,000-hour fixture over a 50,000-hour fixture is much less than the cost of one maintenance visit with a lift.
  • Specify fixtures with tool-free access for drivers and LED modules. If replacing a driver requires special tools and 30 minutes of disassembly, it will not get done promptly.
  • Standardize fixture types. Having fewer fixture types with common parts makes maintenance easier and cheaper.
  • Plan for spare parts. Specify that the supplier provide a spare parts kit — typically 5–10% of key components (drivers, LED modules, lenses).
  • Consider the total cost of ownership, not just the initial purchase price. A quality fixture that lasts a decade with minimal maintenance is cheaper over its life than a budget fixture that needs replacement every few years.

Mistake 9: Over-specifying brands without alternatives

The problem

The specification names a specific brand and model number, with no alternatives allowed. During tender, the contractor comes back with a price that is way over budget because there is no competition. Or the specified product has a long lead time and delays the project. Or the product is discontinued and there is no approved alternative.

Why it happens

Specifying a brand is easy and seems safe. You know exactly what you are getting. But it also eliminates competitive tension and removes flexibility. When the project runs into budget or schedule issues, there are no alternatives to fall back on.

How to avoid it:

  • Write performance specifications instead of brand specifications. Define what the fixture must do (output, beam angle, CRI, UGR, dimming, IP rating, lifespan) rather than what brand it must be.
  • If you want to name a brand as a benchmark, use "or approved equal" language and define what "equal" means in measurable terms.
  • Create a shortlist of 2–3 approved manufacturers for each fixture type. This gives the contractor options while maintaining quality control.
  • Have a clear substitution review process. If a contractor proposes an alternative, there should be a defined process for reviewing and approving (or rejecting) it.
  • Be specific about what parameters are critical and what are flexible. If CRI 90+ is non-negotiable, say so. If the exact finish color can be matched from another brand, that is flexible.
  • Work with suppliers who can provide equivalent alternatives. A good lighting supplier will help you identify comparable products that meet your specification.

Mistake 10: Energy efficiency as an afterthought

The problem

The project is designed, specified, and almost ready to go out to tender, and then someone remembers that the local energy code requires certain efficiency levels. Or the client wants a green building certification. The specification has to be revised at the last minute, costing time and money. Or worse, the fixtures are installed and then fail certification testing.

Why it happens

Energy efficiency is often treated as a box-ticking exercise rather than a design parameter. In the GCC, regulations like SASO 2902 in Saudi Arabia and MOIAT (formerly ESMA) in the UAE have specific requirements for lighting efficacy, and these are only getting stricter.

How to avoid it:

  • Know the local energy codes and standards before you start designing. SASO 2902, MOIAT, Qatar GSAS, Estidama — each has different requirements.
  • Design with efficiency in mind from the beginning, not as a last-minute addition.
  • Specify fixture efficacy (lumens per watt) in addition to total output. Efficacy is the measure of efficiency.
  • Use daylight harvesting and occupancy sensors where appropriate. The most efficient light is the one that is off.
  • Check that control systems are compatible with energy code requirements (e.g., automatic shutoff, dimming, daylight response).
  • Get fixtures that are already certified. If you need SASO or MOIAT certification, make sure the fixtures you specify have it, or budget for the testing and certification process.
  • Do not just look at LED efficacy — look at system efficacy. A high-efficacy LED paired with a bad driver or a bad optic can have poor overall system efficacy.

Mistake 11: Under-designed control systems

The problem

You spend a lot of money on good fixtures, but you pair them with a cheap or basic control system. The dimming is poor, there are not enough scenes, and the system is complicated to use. The result: the lighting never looks as good as it should, and the client is disappointed with the overall experience.

Why it happens

Control systems are often seen as an add-on or an afterthought rather than an integral part of the lighting system. Clients see the fixtures (they are visible), but the control system is hidden, so it is harder to justify the cost. And during value engineering, the control system is often the first thing to get cut.

How to avoid it:

  • Design the control system at the same time as the lighting, not after. The two are inseparable — dimmable fixtures without good dimming are a waste of money.
  • Choose the control protocol based on the project needs, not just the budget. Simple on/off for storage areas, 0–10V for basic dimming, DALI for precise control, KNX/Lutron/Crestron for fully integrated smart systems.
  • Specify control performance requirements, not just equipment lists. How many scenes? What dimming range? What response time? How should the user interface work?
  • Include enough controller capacity for future changes. A system that is maxed out on day one will be a problem when the client wants to add a scene or change a zone.
  • Budget properly for the control system as a meaningful share of the overall lighting package. If you are spending a lot on fixtures, skimping on controls is a false economy.
  • Consider the user experience. The best control system in the world is useless if the people using it cannot figure it out. Wall keypads with clearly labeled scenes are better than app-only control for most commercial projects.

Mistake 12: Skipping commissioning support

The problem

The fixtures are installed, wired up, and turned on, and the project is declared done. But nobody has adjusted the beam angles, set the dimming levels, programmed the scenes, or focused the accent lights. The lighting works, but it does not look right. The design intent is never fully realized, and the space never looks as good as it should.

Why it happens

Commissioning is often not budgeted or scheduled as a distinct phase. The electrical contractor installs the fixtures and considers their job done. The lighting designer may not be contracted for commissioning support, and suppliers may only provide remote commissioning guidance. And by the end of the project, everyone is in a hurry to hand over and move on.

How to avoid it:

  • Include commissioning as a separate line item in the budget. It is a small share of the overall lighting cost and is worth every penny.
  • Schedule commissioning in the project timeline. It takes time — typically 1–5 days depending on project size. It cannot be done in an afternoon.
  • Define what commissioning includes: fixture aiming and focusing, dimming calibration, scene programming, control system testing, and a final walkthrough with the client.
  • Have the lighting designer or a knowledgeable supplier representative on site for commissioning. General electrical contractors do not know how to focus track lights or program lighting scenes.
  • Do commissioning at night, not during the day. You cannot properly evaluate and adjust artificial light levels in a space flooded with daylight.
  • Do a post-occupancy walkthrough a few weeks after handover. Occupants discover issues during use that no one noticed during commissioning.

How to avoid all these mistakes

Most lighting specification mistakes come down to three root causes:

  1. Specifying by appearance rather than performance. A fixture that looks good in a catalog may not perform well in the space.
  2. Not verifying performance before production. Renders and datasheets are not the same as real light.
  3. Considering lighting in isolation. Lighting interacts with architecture, structure, MEP, and controls — it must be coordinated with all of them.

The solution is to write performance-based specifications, verify with samples and mock-ups, and coordinate early and often with all project stakeholders. And it helps to work with suppliers who know what they are doing and can flag potential issues before they become problems.
