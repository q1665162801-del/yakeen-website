The lighting spec checklist we use on every project

12 things we check before we sign off — in the order we check them, with priority levels and the red flags that kill a submission stone dead.

How to use this checklist

Lighting gets value-engineered more than almost anything else in a building. A contractor swaps in a "similar" fixture from a lower-cost brand, the CAD block looks the same, and nobody notices until the lights turn on.

This checklist is our review — the exact thing we run through before we sign off on a product submission. Not in theory. In practice, on real projects, for every fixture.

Priority levels:

  • MUST — non-negotiable. Fails this, we reject the submission.
  • SHOULD — we expect it, and we'll ask why it's missing.
  • NICE — good to have, differentiates good from great.

Use it to evaluate submissions, write performance specs, or call out a contractor who's trying to substitute something low-quality.

1. Photometric data

This is where we start. No IES file = no consideration. Doesn't matter how good the renderings look.

Photometric checklist

  • MUST LM-63 IES file for every fixture variant
  • MUST Tested in an accredited lab (not just self-reported data)
  • SHOULD Beam angle and field angle both defined
  • SHOULD Center beam candlepower (CBCP) specified
  • SHOULD Light output ratio (LOR) documented
  • NICE UGR tables for interior fixtures
  • NICE Spill light and upward light ratio

Red flag: Photometric data that looks "too clean" — perfectly smooth curves, no variation, efficiency above 90%. Real fixtures don't look like that. Always load the IES into DIALux and check it against the stated numbers.

2. Color quality & consistency

Color is where low-quality fixtures are most obviously wrong. Two "3000K" products from different brands can look completely different side by side. On a project with 200 downlights from two different batches? You'll see stripes in the ceiling.

Color specification checklist

  • MUST CCT specified in Kelvin (not "warm white")
  • MUST CRI ≥ 80 general, ≥ 90 for high-end interiors
  • MUST R9 value specified (red rendering — skin tones and food)
  • SHOULD MacAdam ellipse: 3-step or better
  • SHOULD Color shift over life: ≤ 200K over rated life
  • NICE TM-30-15 data available
  • NICE Single-bin or tightly controlled binning

Rule of thumb: For commercial projects with >50 of the same fixture, specify 3-step MacAdam minimum. Anything above 5-step and you'll see color variation between adjacent units. For retail and hospitality, 90+ CRI is not optional — people look wrong under CRI 80.

3. Electrical & control

Control compatibility is where projects go sideways. You spec DALI, the contractor supplies 0-10V drivers, and suddenly nothing dims correctly. Check this early — it's much harder to fix after the walls are up.

  • MUST Dimming protocol: 0-10V, DALI, DMX, TRIAC, or DALI-2? Write it down exactly.
  • MUST Dimming range — minimum dimming level? 1% is good. 10% is not.
  • SHOULD Dimming curve matches the control system (linear / log / square law)
  • SHOULD Power factor ≥ 0.9 for commercial projects
  • SHOULD THD < 20%
  • SHOULD No visible flicker at any dimming level (IEEE 1789)
  • NICE Inrush current documented (for circuit breaker sizing)

Red flag: "DALI compatible" but no DALI-2 certification. DALI-2 is the current standard — plain DALI means the driver is old and may have compatibility issues with modern control systems.

4. Mechanical & thermal

Heat kills LEDs. A fixture with bad thermal management will lose 30% of its output in three years and shift color. The housing isn't just for looks — it's the heatsink.

  • MUST Die-cast aluminum housing (plastic = not architectural grade)
  • MUST IP rating appropriate for the location (IP20 / IP44 / IP65+)
  • SHOULD Junction temperature at rated output documented
  • SHOULD All mounting options available (recessed, surface, suspended, track)
  • SHOULD Weight specified (matters for ceiling structure)
  • NICE Adjustable fixtures: 360° rotation + tilt range stated
  • NICE Custom finishes available with lead time

Rule of thumb: If a fixture feels light for its size, it probably is. And it will probably fail early. Heavier = more aluminum = better heat dissipation = longer life.

5. Certification & compliance

For GCC projects, this isn't optional — it's required for customs clearance and project sign-off. No certificate = your shipment gets stuck at the port.

GCC certification checklist

  • MUST SASO IECEE recognition certificate (Saudi Arabia)
  • MUST G-Mark certification (GCC standard)
  • MUST Certificate covers the exact model, not just "similar"
  • SHOULD MOIAT certification (UAE)
  • SHOULD CB test report (IECEE)
  • SHOULD Local energy efficiency registration
  • NICE RoHS compliance

Red flag: A product company with "certificates" for one model that they apply to their whole product line. The certificate has to match the exact model number and wattage. If it doesn't, it won't pass inspection — and we've seen projects held up for months over this.

6. Warranty & support

Warranty isn't just about replacing dead fixtures. It's about whether the provider will answer the phone when something goes wrong on site.

  • MUST 5 years minimum for specification-grade fixtures
  • MUST Driver included in warranty (80% of failures are drivers)
  • SHOULD L70 lumen maintenance warranty at 50,000 hours
  • SHOULD Color warranty (replacement for bin shift)
  • SHOULD Local support or regional representative
  • NICE Spare parts available 10+ years post-discontinuation
  • NICE Remote commissioning support

Rule of thumb: If a provider only offers 2 years warranty, that's a clue. They don't expect their product to last much longer than that.

7. The mockup (don't skip this)

No specification is complete without seeing it installed. Perfect numbers on a datasheet don't tell you what it actually looks like in that specific ceiling, with that specific paint, at that specific time of day.

For any project above a certain size, we insist on:

  • MUST Mockup of key fixture types in actual ceiling conditions
  • SHOULD Side-by-side comparison if substitutions are proposed
  • SHOULD Sign-off by architect + lighting designer + client
  • NICE Night-time review for exterior and facade fixtures

Mockups cost a few thousand dollars and a week of time. Finding out you hate the light after 500 fixtures are installed? That costs way more. Just do the mockup.
