One Piece or One Hundred: How Custom Orders Are Scoped

A single replacement fixture and a hotel package start from the same drawing but not the same brief. Here is how scope is defined, priced and protected at each quantity.

The same drawing, two orders

A design team sends a detail for a bespoke linear slot. One order is for two pieces, to replace units damaged on a completed floor. Another is for three hundred, across eight floors of a hotel. The drawing is identical. Almost nothing else is.

For two pieces, the work is a one-off build against a drawing, with the emphasis on matching an existing installation that is already on the ceiling. For three hundred, the work is a production exercise: repeatability, jig design, consistency between batches, packing sequence, floor-by-floor labeling, and a sampling process that locks the appearance before the bulk is built.

Scoping means stating which of those two exercises you are asking for, in writing, before a price is produced. Most of the disagreements in custom lighting come from a quotation that assumed one and a project that expected the other.

What changes with quantity

The engineering effort does not scale linearly with the number of pieces. It scales with the number of distinct items, the number of variants, and the amount of coordination the order requires.

  • Distinct items. Ten variants at ten pieces each is a far larger order to manage than one hundred identical pieces.
  • Variant differences. A different length, finish, color temperature or beam angle is a separate line, a separate drawing and a separate label.
  • Sampling requirement. A single-piece order can be approved on delivery. A package normally needs a sample or mock-up approved before the balance is built.
  • Inspection requirement. Larger projects often carry a formal inspection and release process; smaller ones usually do not.
  • Delivery sequencing. A package has to arrive in the order the site can install it, which is a planning task rather than a packing task.

This is why a quantity increase can change the price per piece in either direction. Unit cost usually falls, but the coordination, sampling and documentation load rises. A quotation that only reflects the first effect is not a complete quotation.

Tooling, jigs and repeatability

Custom does not automatically mean unique. A bespoke profile, a specific bezel, a particular diffuser and a made-to-length body are all repeatable once the geometry is fixed. What makes them repeatable is a jig, a drawing and a check.

On a package, the first article is the reference. It is measured, photographed, and compared against the drawing before anything else is built. Every subsequent piece is then checked against the first article rather than against an interpretation of the drawing. This is the simplest and most effective control on a custom order, and it is the step that gets skipped when a project is compressed.

Tolerance also has to be agreed in numbers rather than words. "Match the sample" is not a tolerance. A stated dimension with a stated allowance is. The same applies to finish: a powder coat is matched to a sample panel or to a reference, and both sides know which.

Sampling and mock-ups

Sampling is the cheapest risk reduction available on a custom order, and it is routinely left out of the program. Three levels are worth distinguishing:

  • Material and finish sample. A small panel or a short section to confirm color, texture and gloss. Fast, and enough to sign off a finish.
  • Working sample. A complete fixture, built to the drawing, tested for output, color and dimming. Appropriate for a first order, a new detail, or any project where the appearance has to be signed off before quantity is committed.
  • Site mock-up. One detail built into the actual ceiling or wall build-up on site, by the actual trades. This is the only way to prove the interface between fixture and substrate, and it is where most of the real problems are found.

For a single piece, the working sample and the delivered item are often the same thing, which is fine provided the client understands that approval happens at delivery. For a package, the sample approval has to be a formal gate, with a written release before the balance is built.

Packing, labeling and site sequence

On a large order, packing is part of the engineering. A fixture that arrives undamaged but unlabeled creates a site problem that costs more than the packing saved.

The useful standard is that every carton identifies the item, the variant, the quantity, the floor or zone it belongs to, and the drawing revision it was built to. Where the project has an installation sequence — floor by floor, or zone by zone — the packing should follow that sequence rather than the production sequence.

Protection matters as much as labeling. Powder-coated bodies travel badly against each other, diffusers scratch, and stone-mounted sleeves are easily chipped. Individual wrapping on finished items is not a luxury on a package order; it is the difference between a snag list and a reorder.

Certification and documentation

Documentation should be scoped at the same time as the product, because the two are quoted together. A package order usually carries a submittal requirement: datasheets, photometric files, installation instructions, finish references and compliance documents, all in the format the consultant has asked for.

For projects in Saudi Arabia, SASO 2902 certification is held at factory level for the product families we supply, and the specific model, variant and quantity are confirmed at quotation. That distinction matters when a document is being assembled for a consultant: certification is a property of the product family, and the scope of the order is what the quotation defines.

Where a project requires test reports or a specific declaration, ask for them at enquiry stage rather than at delivery. Documents that have to be produced after an order is placed are the documents that delay a handover.

What to put in the enquiry

Scope checklist

  • The drawing or reference image, with the detail that matters marked
  • Quantity by variant, and the total number of distinct items
  • Whether the requirement is one piece, a replacement run, or a full package
  • Sample or mock-up requirement, and who approves it
  • Finish reference, and the tolerance you will accept
  • Packing and labeling requirement, including floor or zone marking
  • Documentation set required for submittal
  • Delivery sequence, as distinct from a delivery date

None of this requires a complete specification. It requires an honest statement of the scope, so that the quotation can answer the question that is actually being asked. A single piece and a full package are both work we take on; the difference is in how the order is built, checked and packed, and that difference should be visible in the quotation from the first line.
