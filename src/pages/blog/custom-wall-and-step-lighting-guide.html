Custom Wall & Step Lighting for Stone, Stairs and Wet Zones

These are the details that get built before the light arrives. Recess depths, core sizes, drainage, IP ratings and finishes for stone walls, stair flights and wet areas.

A detail, not a product

Wall and step lights are small, cheap relative to the rest of a lighting package, and almost always the first item to become a problem on site. The reason is sequencing. A recessed wall light is not installed when the electrician arrives; it is installed when the stone is being fixed, by the stone contractor, using a cut-out that was set out months earlier from a drawing that may or may not have been coordinated.

That makes these fixtures a detail problem before they are a lighting problem. The question is not only how much light comes out, but how the fixture sits in a 30mm stone panel, where its driver lives, how water drains away from it, and who is responsible for the hole.

Custom work is normal here because the architecture is normal: 20mm stone with a 15mm mortar bed, 40mm solid stone, tiled risers, timber cladding, plaster returns, and the occasional step nosing that was never designed to carry a fitting at all.

Recessing into stone and plaster

Four dimensions decide whether a recessed wall light can be built into a given wall:

  • Recess depth available. Measured from the finished face back to the structural wall or the edge of the blockwork. A 55mm body does not fit into a 40mm stone panel over a 15mm bed.
  • Core or cut-out size. The hole the stone contractor has to form, and the tolerance they can hold. Stone cores cleanly; a chipped edge on a polished face is not repairable.
  • Body diameter and bezel. A fixture with a 60mm bezel will cover a rough core; a 45mm bezel with a 1mm shadow line will not.
  • Fixation method. Spring clips suit plasterboard; stone needs a sleeve, a grout-in collar, or a rear fixing bracket that is set before the panel goes up.

Where the available depth cannot take a recessed body, the design either moves to a surface-mounted fitting or to a shallow body with a remote driver. Both are legitimate answers, and both have to be decided before the stone is cut — not after.

Steps, risers and stringers

Stair lighting is where safety and composition meet. The functional requirement is to make the edge of each tread readable; the architectural requirement is to do that without turning the stair into a runway.

  • Riser recessed. A small fixture set into the riser, throwing a low pool of light across the tread. The most common detail, and the most forgiving, because the fitting is protected from view and from impact.
  • Tread recessed. A slot or a small fitting set into the tread surface, usually near the nosing. Effective, but the detail has to survive foot traffic and cleaning.
  • Stringer or wall-mounted. Fittings set into the side wall or the stringer, lighting across the flight. Good where the risers are stone and cannot be cut, and good for open stairs.
  • Handrail integrated. Linear or small downlights built into the underside of a handrail. Precise, discreet, and demanding of the joinery.

Two numbers matter on every stair: the pitch of the flight and the nosing line. The fixture has to be positioned so the light falls on the tread and the nosing, not on the riser face below the fixture. On a steep or spiral flight, the geometry changes from step to step, and a single detail repeated at every step will not produce even lighting.

Wet zones, IP ratings and drainage

Wet areas are where specification shortcuts show up first. Water does not need to flood a fixture to damage it; condensation inside a sealed body is enough. In GCC projects the relevant locations are bathrooms, ablution areas, pool surrounds, spa and steam rooms, kitchen back-of-house, and covered external terraces.

The IP rating has to match the location, and it has to survive installation. A fitting rated for jets of water will still fail if it is installed with a damaged gasket or with the cable entry pointing upwards. Three practical rules apply:

  • Drain the detail. Any recess should have a fall away from the body, or a weep path, so water cannot stand against the gasket.
  • Keep cable entries low or sealed. A gland at the top of a fitting collects water; the same gland at the bottom does not.
  • Check the driver location. Remote drivers in a wet ceiling void need their own protection, and a driver in a bathroom ceiling needs to be accessible for replacement.

Where a wall light sits inside a shower enclosure, the fixture rating, the sealing method and the backing detail all have to be agreed with the waterproofing trade. This is usually the point at which a custom body with a specific gasket and a specific sleeve is the only way to keep the waterproofing membrane continuous.

Cuts, cores and the building sequence

The single biggest cause of failed wall and step lighting is that the hole is made before the fixture is chosen, or the fixture arrives after the hole is made. Both produce the same result: a bezel that does not cover, or a core that has to be reworked in a finished wall.

The sequence that works is straightforward. Confirm the fixture body and the core size first, issue a setting-out drawing to the stone or plaster contractor, provide a template or a sample body, and inspect the first core before the contractor proceeds with the rest. On projects with hundreds of steps or a long stone corridor, that first core is the whole quality control system.

Allow for the fact that stone panels are often fixed from the bottom of a wall upwards, and the electrician may not have access to the rear of the panel afterwards. First-fix cabling has to be in place, with enough slack to reach the fitting position, before the panel is bedded.

Finishes and coastal corrosion

Wall and step lights sit close to the surfaces people touch and clean, so finish quality is visible in a way that a ceiling fitting never is. Powder-coated aluminum in a matte finish is the practical default. Polished or brushed stainless is common on coastal projects, and needs a grade that suits the salt exposure rather than a decorative grade chosen for appearance.

On the Gulf coast, salt-laden air and regular washing combine to attack fixings, gaskets and any unprotected edge. Screws of a different alloy to the body will corrode first and seize. Specify the fixing material along with the finish; it is a small line item that determines whether the fitting can be serviced in five years.

Documenting the requirement

Send wall sections at each condition, the stone or tile thickness and bed depth, the stair section with the nosing line, the wet-zone waterproofing detail, and the finish you want. Where the wall build-up is not yet fixed, say so — it changes the body depth that can be offered.
