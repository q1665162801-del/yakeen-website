Why downlight specification matters

Downlights are the workhorse of architectural lighting. They are everywhere — in ceilings of hotel lobbies, retail floors, office corridors, and residential living rooms. And yet, most projects treat them as a commodity: pick a wattage, pick a color, done.

That is a mistake. The difference between a well-specified downlight and a budget one is not subtle. Walk into a space and you can feel it within ten seconds: Is the ceiling clean and uniform, or a patchwork of mismatched holes? Is the light comfortable, or does it make you squint? Do colors look right, or washed out and gray?

After specifying thousands of downlights across GCC projects, we have learned that every decision matters — from the cutout size to the depth of the reflector to the type of driver. This guide walks you through the key decisions and tells you what to look for on a spec sheet (and what the spec sheet leaves out).

Cutout sizes and aperture options

The first decision is also the most visible: how big is the hole in the ceiling? Downlight apertures range from 50mm micro-downlights to 200mm+ high-wattage fixtures. The choice depends on ceiling height, desired light output, and architectural intent.

Common aperture sizes and typical uses

55 – 75mm
Micro / Mini. Low ceilings, display cases, cove details
90 – 110mm
Standard. Most commercial and hospitality applications
125 – 150mm
Mid-size. Higher ceilings, more lumens needed
175 – 200mm+
Large. High-bay, lobbies, atriums

For specification-grade projects, the trend has been toward smaller apertures. A 90mm cutout with a 15W COB LED delivering 1200+ lumens covers most general lighting applications in 2.7m to 3.2m ceiling heights. The smaller hole reads as more precise and architectural.

Beyond size, there are three trim styles to consider:

  • Trimless (plaster-in). The fixture flange is embedded in the plaster or gypsum board for a completely flush, invisible look. The most architectural option, but requires careful installation coordination.
  • Fixed trim. A visible metal ring sits on the ceiling surface. Clean and straightforward, available in various finishes.
  • Adjustable / gimbal. The light engine can tilt and rotate, allowing you to aim the beam. Useful for wall washing or accent lighting, but generally larger and less glare-controlled.

Pitfall: inconsistent cutout sizes on site

Trimless downlights live or die by the precision of the ceiling opening. A cutout that is 5mm too large leaves a visible gap. Always specify the exact cutout diameter (not just "approximate") and provide the template to the ceiling contractor before they start cutting. On GCC projects, we recommend a site mock-up before full installation.

UGR and glare control

Glare is the single most important quality differentiator between specification-grade downlights and budget ones. A cheap downlight with a bright visible LED source will make a space feel harsh and low-quality, even at low light levels.

UGR (Unified Glare Rating) is the standard metric, but it is a room-level calculation, not a fixture rating. That said, fixture design is the biggest single factor. The key elements of glare control in a downlight are:

  • Deep recess (recess depth). The deeper the LED source is set back into the fixture, the lower the glare. Look for a recess depth of at least 30mm for UGR<19 performance. Spec-grade fixtures often have 50mm+ recess depth.
  • Reflector design. A well-designed COB reflector shapes the beam while keeping the source hidden from normal viewing angles. Cheap spun-aluminum reflectors have visible striations and poor cutoff.
  • Baffles and blackened interiors. A black baffle at the aperture absorbs stray light and makes the fixture visually recede. Specify "black baffle" or "darklight" for the most comfortable appearance.
  • Cutoff angle. The angle at which the source becomes visible. A 60° cutoff angle means the source is invisible from below 60° from vertical — standard for good glare control. Look for "60° cutoff" or better on the spec sheet.

For most architectural applications, target UGR<19 for task areas and UGR<22 for circulation. If the spec sheet does not mention UGR, the fixture probably does not have good glare control. Always request IES files and run a quick DIALux calculation to verify.

CRI and color quality

Color rendering is what makes people, food, materials, and merchandise look good. It is also the most commonly under-specified parameter in downlights.

The minimum for any specification-grade project is CRI 90 (Ra 90) with R9 > 50. Do not accept anything lower. Here is why:

  • CRI 80 is the bare minimum for basic commercial, but it is noticeably poor in side-by-side comparison. Skin tones look gray, reds look dull, wood looks washed out.
  • CRI 90 is the spec-grade standard. Colors are vivid and accurate. This is what you want for hospitality, retail, high-end residential, and any space where visual quality matters.
  • CRI 95+ is for the most demanding applications — art galleries, museums, high-end fashion retail, and premium hospitality. The difference from CRI 90 is subtle but real, especially with saturated colors and skin tones.
  • R9 matters. A fixture can have Ra 90 and R9 of 0. R9 measures saturated red rendering, which directly affects how skin, food, and red objects look. Always specify R9 > 50 as a minimum.

Color consistency is equally important. Two downlights with the same rated CCT can look different when installed next to each other. The industry standard for spec-grade is 3-step MacAdam ellipse (3 SDCM). If the spec sheet says "5-step" or does not specify, expect visible color variation across a ceiling — especially with warmer color temperatures.

Beam angles and optics

Beam angle determines how widely the light spreads. Downlights typically offer several beam angle options, from narrow spot (15°) to wide flood (60°+).

Beam angle guide

15° – 20° Narrow spot
Accent lighting, high ceilings, highlighting objects
24° – 36° Medium / Flood
General ambient lighting, most common
40° – 60° Wide flood
Low ceilings, uniform ambient, wall washing

The right beam angle depends on ceiling height, fixture spacing, and the desired light distribution. As a rough guide for general ambient lighting:

  • 2.6m – 3.0m ceiling: 36° beam, spaced 1.5m – 2.0m apart
  • 3.0m – 4.0m ceiling: 24° beam, spaced 2.0m – 2.5m apart
  • 4.0m – 6.0m ceiling: 15° – 20° beam, spaced 2.5m – 3.5m apart

These are starting points. Always verify with a lighting calculation. And pay attention to how beam angle is measured: it should be the full-width half-maximum (FWHM) angle, which is the industry standard. Some manufacturers cite "field angle" which is wider and less meaningful.

Color temperature

Color temperature sets the mood of a space. The choice between 2700K and 4000K is not a "brightness" decision — it is an atmosphere decision.

  • 2700K — Warm, intimate, residential-feeling. Standard for luxury hotels, fine dining, high-end residential. Feels like incandescent.
  • 3000K — Warm white, versatile. The workhorse for retail, casual dining, mid-market hospitality, and general commercial.
  • 3500K — Neutral white. Clean, modern, alert. Popular for offices, healthcare, and contemporary retail.
  • 4000K — Cool white. Energetic, clinical. For offices, laboratories, and industrial spaces. Generally too cool for hospitality or residential.
  • 5000K+ — Daylight. Stark, blue-heavy. Use only for specialized industrial or outdoor applications.

For most GCC projects, we see 2700K and 3000K as the dominant choices. The regional preference leans warm, especially in hospitality and residential. A common mistake is specifying 4000K because it "seems brighter" — at the same lumen output, 4000K does appear brighter visually, but it also feels colder and less inviting.

Dimming protocols

Dimming performance makes or breaks the user experience. A downlight that flickers or drops out at low levels will ruin an otherwise great lighting design. The dimming protocol you specify determines how smoothly the fixture dims and how deep it can go.

  • DALI. The standard for commercial and hospitality projects. Digital, addressable, smooth dimming down to 1%. DALI-2 is the latest version with better interoperability. Always specify DALI-2 for new projects.
  • 0-10V. Analog, simple, widely supported. Dimming range typically 1% – 100%. Good for basic dimming, less precise than DALI. Common in offices and retail.
  • TRIAC / ELV. Mainly for residential and small commercial. Performance varies widely by driver quality. Good TRIAC drivers can dim to 5% or lower; cheap ones flicker and drop out at 20%.
  • Non-dim. On/off only. Use only for areas where dimming is never needed (storage, mechanical rooms).

Pitfall: dimming performance at low end

The spec sheet says "dimmable to 1%" but in practice, the fixture starts flickering at 10%. This is the most common dimming complaint. The issue is usually a cheap driver paired with a mismatched controller. Always request a dimming sample and test it at low levels with the actual control system before committing to a full order.

IP ratings and wet locations

IP (Ingress Protection) rating tells you how well the fixture is sealed against dust and water. For downlights, the relevant ratings are:

  • IP20. Indoor dry locations only. The standard for most downlights used in offices, hotels, retail, and residential.
  • IP44. Protected against splashing water from any direction. Suitable for bathrooms above the shower zone and areas with occasional moisture.
  • IP65. Dust-tight and protected against water jets. Required for shower areas, steam rooms, and outdoor-sheltered locations. An IP65 downlight has a sealed lens and a gasket between the trim and the ceiling.

For GCC projects, IP65 downlights are commonly specified for mosque ablution areas, hotel bathrooms, outdoor covered walkways, and kitchens. Note that "IP65 rated" is not enough — the fixture must be installed with the correct gasket and sealing method to maintain the rating.

Materials and thermal management

LED life is thermal. The cooler the LED runs, the longer it lasts and the better it maintains its light output and color. Thermal management is where budget downlights cut corners most visibly.

What to look for:

  • Die-cast aluminum heat sink. The fixture body should be a substantial die-cast aluminum piece that acts as both structure and heat spreader. Thin stamped steel or plastic housings are a red flag.
  • Separate driver (remote or integral). A separate driver means the heat from the driver electronics is not cooking the LED. Remote drivers (mounted above the ceiling) are better for thermal performance but add installation complexity. Integral drivers are simpler but need good thermal design.
  • LM-80 tested LED source. LM-80 is the industry standard for long-term lumen maintenance testing. Look for LM-80 data with at least 6000 hours of testing at the rated current and temperature.
  • L70 rating at 25°C ambient. The L70 rating tells you how long until the fixture reaches 70% of initial output. 50,000 hours is standard for spec-grade. Always check the ambient temperature the rating is based on — 25°C is standard, but in a hot ceiling plenum in Riyadh summer, the actual ambient can be 40°C+ and the life will be shorter.

For finish quality, look for powder-coated aluminum trims in matte white or matte black. The finish should be smooth and consistent. Budget fixtures often have cheap paint that yellows over time, especially at warm color temperatures.

Common specification pitfalls

From our team's years of downlight specification work, these are the mistakes we see most often:

  1. Specifying by wattage alone. "Give me a 20W downlight" tells you almost nothing. Two 20W downlights can have wildly different light output, beam quality, and color rendering. Always specify by lumens, CRI, beam angle, and UGR — not watts.
  2. Ignoring color consistency. A ceiling with mismatched color temperatures looks terrible. Always specify 3-step MacAdam and require the manufacturer to bin the LEDs from the same batch for the project.
  3. Forgetting the ceiling type. A downlight designed for gypsum board may not work in a concrete ceiling or a suspended T-bar ceiling. Check the installation method and ceiling thickness before specifying.
  4. Underestimating plenum clearance. Some downlights need 150mm+ of clearance above the ceiling. If the plenum is shallow, you need a low-profile fixture. Always check the fixture depth against the available plenum space.
  5. Not testing dimming on site. Dimming compatibility issues are common and difficult to fix after installation. Always do a mock-up with the actual control system before full delivery.

Downlight selection checklist

Specification checklist

  • ☐ Aperture size matches ceiling design and available plenum depth
  • ☐ Trim style — trimless, fixed, or adjustable — appropriate for the application
  • ☐ Light output (lumens) meets calculated requirements, not just wattage
  • ☐ CRI Ra 90+ minimum, with R9 > 50 specified
  • ☐ 3-step MacAdam color consistency or better
  • ☐ UGR target defined for each space (verify with IES files)
  • ☐ Beam angle matched to ceiling height and spacing
  • ☐ Dimming protocol compatible with the control system (DALI-2, 0-10V, TRIAC)
  • ☐ IP rating appropriate for the location (IP20 dry, IP65 wet)
  • ☐ Die-cast aluminum housing with proper heat sinking
  • ☐ LM-80 tested LED source with L70 50,000h rating
  • ☐ IES files provided for lighting calculation verification
  • ☐ Warranty — 5 years minimum for spec-grade
  • ☐ SASO / MOIAT certification for GCC market access
  • ☐ Mock-up sample ordered and approved before bulk production
