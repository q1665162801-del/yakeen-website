Why cove lighting is worth getting right

Cove lighting — also called indirect lighting or hidden lighting — is one of the most effective tools in architectural lighting design. When it works, it is invisible: the ceiling or wall seems to glow from an unseen source, bathing the space in soft, even, comfortable light. When it does not work, it is the most obvious mistake in the room: visible dots of light, uneven brightness, color shifts along the run, or a weak glow that barely registers.

Good cove lighting does several things at once:

  • Raises perceived ceiling height. A lit ceiling makes a room feel taller and more spacious.
  • Provides soft ambient light. Reflected light is more comfortable and evenly distributed than direct light.
  • Adds visual depth. Layers of light — direct + cove + accent — make a space feel rich and dimensional.
  • Creates mood. Dimmed cove lighting transforms a space from functional to atmospheric.

The problem is that cove lighting is often treated as an afterthought. "Just stick some LED strip in the cove," the thinking goes. But that approach almost never produces a good result. Cove lighting is a system — the fixture, the cove geometry, the surface finish, and the controls all work together. Get any one of them wrong and the whole thing falls flat.

LED strip vs. linear fixture

The first big decision is what type of light source to use in the cove. There are two main options, and the choice determines everything else.

Flexible LED strip

Flexible LED strip light (also called LED tape) is the most common approach — and the most problematic. A roll of LED strip is cheap, easy to install, and can follow curves. But it has serious limitations for architectural specification:

Pros:

  • Low material cost
  • Easy to cut and install on site
  • Follows curves and irregular shapes
  • Available in many color temperatures and RGB options

Cons:

  • Poor heat dissipation. Adhesive-backed strip stuck to aluminum or plastic channel does not cool the LEDs well. Life expectancy drops significantly, especially at high drive currents.
  • Visible dotting. If the strip is visible in the cove (even as a reflection), individual LED dots are obvious with standard 60 or 120 LEDs/m strip.
  • Color inconsistency. Budget LED strip is notoriously variable in color temperature. Two reels from the same batch can be noticeably different.
  • Short warranty. Most LED strip comes with a 1-2 year warranty, if any. Not acceptable for spec-grade projects.

Linear LED fixture

A purpose-built linear LED fixture is an aluminum extrusion with a PCB, LEDs, and optics designed specifically for cove or indirect lighting applications.

Pros:

  • Excellent thermal management. The aluminum body acts as a heat sink, keeping LEDs cool and ensuring long life.
  • Superior color consistency. Spec-grade linear fixtures use binned LEDs (3-step MacAdam) for consistent color across the entire run.
  • Better optics. Available with asymmetric or symmetric beam patterns specifically designed for cove lighting. Delivers more light where you want it.
  • Longer life and warranty. 50,000+ hour L70 life and 5-year warranties are standard for spec-grade.
  • Seamless joints. Good linear systems have end-to-end LED alignment for continuous light without dark spots at joints.

Cons:

  • Higher initial cost (2-5x the cost of LED strip)
  • Rigid — cannot follow curves (some systems have flexible connectors)
  • Requires more precise planning and installation

Our recommendation

For specification-grade architectural projects, always use purpose-built linear LED fixtures for cove lighting. The cost difference is real, but the performance gap is enormous — in color quality, uniformity, reliability, and lifespan. LED strip is acceptable only for budget residential projects or for applications where the strip is completely hidden and the quality bar is low.

Cove geometry and reflector design

The shape and size of the cove is just as important as the light source itself. A poorly designed cove will produce a poor result even with the best fixtures.

Cove depth (height)

The cove needs to be deep enough to completely hide the light source from normal viewing angles. If you can see the LEDs or the fixture body from anywhere in the room, the cove is too shallow.

  • Minimum cove depth: 120mm for small coves with low-profile fixtures
  • Recommended cove depth: 150mm – 200mm for most applications
  • Deep coves (300mm+): Used in high-ceiling spaces and for dramatic effects

The depth requirement also depends on how far the cove is from the viewer. A cove in a low ceiling (2.6m) needs more depth to hide the source than one in a high ceiling (4.0m) because the viewing angle is steeper.

Cove width

Wider coves give the light more room to spread before hitting the ceiling or wall, resulting in a softer, more gradual gradient. Narrow coves produce a tighter, more defined light line.

  • Narrow cove (100-150mm): Creates a defined line of light. Good for contemporary interiors and feature details.
  • Medium cove (150-250mm): The most versatile. Produces a good balance between definition and diffusion.
  • Wide cove (250mm+): Creates a very soft, broad wash of light. Good for ambient lighting in large spaces.

Reflector surfaces

What the light bounces off matters enormously. The ceiling (or wall) that the cove illuminates is effectively part of the lighting system.

  • White or off-white ceiling. The standard choice. Reflects 70-85% of the light for maximum efficiency.
  • Colored ceiling. Tints the light with the color of the ceiling. Can be a beautiful effect, but you lose significant light output. A dark ceiling may reflect only 20-30% of the light.
  • Textured surface. Diffuses the light further for softer gradients. But be careful with heavily textured surfaces — they can create unexpected shadow patterns.
  • Glossy or mirrored surfaces. Produce sharp reflections that may show the individual light sources. Generally avoid for cove lighting unless the source is very well diffused.

The inside of the cove itself should also be considered. A white or light-colored interior reflects more light upward and increases efficiency. A dark interior absorbs light but can help conceal the source. Specify the cove interior finish explicitly — do not leave it to chance.

Color temperature consistency

Color consistency is the #1 complaint about cove lighting. When you have a long run of cove — say, surrounding an entire room — even small color differences between segments become immediately obvious. The eye is extremely good at detecting color differences across a uniform white surface like a ceiling.

What causes color inconsistency in cove lighting?

  • Mixed LED batches. Different reels or segments of LED strip from different production batches have slightly different color temperatures.
  • Different fixture types. Mixing brands or even different product lines from the same brand can produce different colors.
  • Temperature differences. LEDs shift color as they heat up. If part of the run runs hotter (e.g., because it is in an enclosed section), that section will be a different color.
  • Dimming level. Cheap LEDs shift color when dimmed, usually becoming greener or more purple at low levels. Good fixtures maintain consistent color across the dimming range.

How to ensure color consistency:

  1. Specify 3-step MacAdam (3 SDCM) minimum. This ensures that any two LEDs from the same batch will be indistinguishable in color to most observers. For the most demanding applications, specify 2-step.
  2. Order all fixtures from a single production batch. Ask the manufacturer to confirm that all fixtures for the project come from the same LED bin and production run.
  3. Use the same fixture type throughout. Do not mix different products or different brands in the same visible run.
  4. Test at full brightness and at dimmed levels. Color consistency at 100% does not guarantee consistency at 20%. Always verify at the expected operating levels.

Dimming depth and performance

Cove lighting is almost always dimmed — that is the whole point of ambient mood lighting. But dimming performance is where many cove lighting installations fail.

Common dimming problems in cove lighting:

  • Drop-out at low levels. The light cuts out entirely at 10-20% brightness instead of dimming smoothly to a low level.
  • Visible stepping. The brightness jumps in visible steps rather than changing smoothly.
  • Flicker. Flicker at low dimming levels is common with cheap drivers.
  • Color shift. The color temperature changes as the light dims — often becoming greenish or purplish.

For smooth, deep dimming of cove lighting:

  • Use DALI or DALI-2 dimming. Digital dimming is inherently smoother than analog. DALI systems can dim to 1% or lower with high-quality drivers.
  • Specify high-quality drivers. The driver is more important than the LEDs for dimming quality. Mean Well, Philips Xitanium, Tridonic, and similar brands are reliable. Avoid no-name drivers.
  • Consider warm-dim (dim-to-warm) technology. Some fixtures are designed to shift toward warmer color temperatures as they dim, mimicking the behavior of incandescent and halogen lamps. This creates a very natural, comfortable dimming experience that is especially popular in hospitality.
  • Test dimming on a sample before committing. Always test a full fixture+dimmer combination at low levels before ordering the full quantity.

Common specification mistakes

After specifying cove lighting for dozens of GCC projects, these are the mistakes we see most often:

  1. Under-sizing the cove. The architect designs a 60mm cove and expects it to produce a beautiful wash of light. It will not. The minimum is 100-120mm, and 150mm+ is much better. Always review cove dimensions during the design development phase.
  2. Using cheap LED strip. The cost savings on strip are negligible compared to the cost of the cove construction. Spend a little more on good fixtures and get a result that is actually worth having.
  3. Forgetting about service access. Cove fixtures need to be serviced eventually. If the cove is completely inaccessible, a single failed driver means a major repair. Design in access panels or use fixtures that can be serviced from below.
  4. Ignoring the ceiling finish. A dark ceiling will absorb 70%+ of the cove light. If the design calls for a dark ceiling, you need much more powerful fixtures — or you need to rethink the cove approach.
  5. Mixing fixture types in the same run. Using two different products (or even the same product from different shipments) in one continuous cove almost guarantees visible color mismatch.
  6. Not accounting for corners and transitions. Inside corners, outside corners, and changes in direction are where cove lighting goes wrong most often. Make sure the system has proper corner connectors that maintain light output and color consistency through the turn.
  7. Overdriving LED strip. It is common practice to run LED strip at higher than rated current to get more light. This dramatically reduces life and shifts color. Never overdrive — use a higher-output product instead.

How to guarantee a good result

There is no substitute for seeing it in person. Here is the process we recommend for critical cove lighting installations:

  1. Order a sample segment. Get a 1m sample of the exact fixture and color temperature you plan to specify.
  2. Build a mock-up cove. Have the contractor build a 1m-2m section of the actual cove design — same dimensions, same ceiling finish, same everything.
  3. Install and evaluate. Install the sample fixture, connect it to the actual dimming system, and evaluate at full brightness and at dimmed levels. Look for color uniformity, source concealment, and light distribution.
  4. Get sign-off. Have the designer, client, and contractor all approve the mock-up before proceeding with the full order.

A mock-up costs a few hundred dollars and a few days of time. It prevents tens of thousands of dollars in rework and weeks of delay on site. For any project where cove lighting is a key design element, it is always worth doing.

Cove lighting specification checklist

Specification checklist

  • ☐ Light source type — linear LED fixture (spec-grade) or LED strip (budget only)
  • ☐ Cove depth sufficient to hide source from all normal viewing angles (150mm+ recommended)
  • ☐ Cove width matched to desired light spread
  • ☐ Cove interior finish specified (white, painted, etc.)
  • ☐ Ceiling/wall surface reviewed for reflectance and color
  • ☐ CRI Ra 90+ with R9 > 50 for spec-grade applications
  • ☐ 3-step MacAdam (3 SDCM) color consistency minimum
  • ☐ Single production batch confirmed with manufacturer
  • ☐ Dimming protocol — DALI-2 preferred for deep, smooth dimming
  • ☐ Dimming depth verified — 1% for DALI, test at low levels
  • ☐ Warm-dim specified if incandescent-like dimming is desired
  • ☐ Corner connectors available and maintain light output through turns
  • ☐ Service access considered and provided
  • ☐ Warranty — 5 years minimum for spec-grade linear fixtures
  • ☐ SASO / MOIAT certification for GCC market access
  • ☐ Site mock-up planned and approved before full installation
