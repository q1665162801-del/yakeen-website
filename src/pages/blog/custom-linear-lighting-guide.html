Custom Linear Lighting: Lengths, Curves and Seamless Joints

A linear run is a drawing problem before it is a lighting problem. Here is how lengths, joints, radii and power are resolved so the finished line looks continuous.

Why a linear run is a drawing problem

A linear fixture is the most architectural product in a lighting package, and the least forgiving. A downlight can move 50mm and nobody notices. A linear run cannot: the line either lands on the joint in the ceiling, or it does not.

That is why the useful conversation about custom linear lighting is not about profiles or wattages. It is about the geometry of the architecture: where the line starts, where it ends, how it turns a corner, what it sits inside, and what happens at every interruption. Everything else follows from those decisions.

In GCC interiors these questions come up constantly. Long majlis ceilings, hotel ballrooms, retail perimeters, airport and office circulation, and villa corridors all invite continuous lines. The same projects also carry heavy services: ducts, chilled water, sprinkler runs and structural beams that interrupt exactly the path a designer wants to draw.

Setting run lengths from the architecture

The starting point is a dimensioned reflected ceiling plan, not a preferred length. Measure the actual opening between the two fixed points — wall to wall, wall to column, column to column — and give the tolerance you can live with at each end.

Three end conditions account for most of the detail work:

  • Wall to wall. The run either finishes flush into a shadow gap, stops on a surface flange, or dies into a plaster return. Each has a different installation order, and the plaster has to be built to suit.
  • Wall to nothing. A run that simply stops in the ceiling plane needs a deliberate end cap. A cut profile with a raw edge is visible from below and reads as unfinished work.
  • Run inside a slot or cove. The slot depth and width are fixed by the ceiling design, so the profile has to suit the slot rather than the other way round.

Length is also a transport and handling constraint. A 6m section cannot travel in a standard lift or turn a tight stair. Where a run is longer than the access allows, the joint has to be designed into the line at a point where it will be least visible — typically on a construction module, not in the middle of the most-photographed wall.

What a seamless joint actually means

"Seamless" is used loosely in the market. In practice it means three different things, and they cost differently:

  • Invisible in service. Sections are joined with a machined connector and a shared diffuser. The joint is undetectable from normal viewing distance under normal light levels, and there is no dark band.
  • Invisible when lit only. The joint is slightly visible when the fixture is switched off, and disappears when the LED is running. This is common in cove and slot details where the diffuser is recessed.
  • Mechanically continuous. The aluminum body and the light engine run continuously, but a joint line remains visible in the finish. Acceptable in industrial or service areas, rarely acceptable in hospitality.

The joint quality is decided by three things: the accuracy of the cut on the profile ends, the rigidity of the connector that bridges them, and whether the diffuser is continuous or butted.

On long runs, thermal expansion matters as well. Aluminium moves with temperature, and a continuous line fixed rigidly at every point will either bow or crack a diffuser. Good detailing fixes the profile at one point and allows the rest of the run to move.

Curved runs: radius, chord and segment

Curves are where catalogs usually end and custom work begins. A curved line is defined by a radius, and the radius determines whether the profile can be rolled, bent, or built from straight segments.

What to send for a curved run

Radius
Measured to the face of the ceiling or the centerline of the run, stated explicitly
Arc length
Or the start and end points plus the radius, from which the arc can be derived
Profile depth
Deep sections resist tight radii; shallow sections bend further before distortion
Diffuser direction
Inward or outward radius changes which face stretches and which compresses

A tight radius on a deep profile will distort the diffuser channel, and the LED strip inside will bunch on the inner face. Where the radius is too tight for a continuous bend, the line is built as a series of short segments with mitered or welded joints. Faceted curves can look convincing from below on a large radius, and look wrong on a small one.

Diffusers and the quality of the line

The diffuser is what people actually see. It determines whether the run reads as a uniform line of light or as a series of bright dots with dark gaps between them. The choice depends on how far the viewer is from the fixture and at what angle they see it.

  • Opal extruded diffuser. The default for general linear lighting. Even, soft, and effective at hiding pitch, provided the diffuser sits far enough from the LED.
  • Micro-prismatic diffuser. Better glare control where the fixture is seen at low angles, and useful in offices and galleries.
  • Recessed or deep-set diffuser. The LED sits back inside the profile, so the source is not visible from the side. This is the detail that makes a run look architectural rather than applied.
  • Asymmetric or wall-wash optic. Where the run is meant to graze a surface rather than light a floor, the optic does the work and the diffuser is secondary.

Color consistency along a run is as important as output. A 6m line built from three different LED batches can show a visible step in color temperature. Specify a single bin and a single MacAdam step for the whole run, and treat long runs as one production item rather than several.

Power, drivers and voltage drop

Long runs create a problem that short fixtures never face: voltage drop. A 24V feed that works over 2m will lose brightness at the far end of a 12m run, and the loss is progressive rather than obvious until the line is photographed.

The practical answers are to feed from both ends, to split the run into separately fed sections, or to move to a constant-current engine with remote drivers. Which one is right depends on the length, the load per meter and where the driver can physically live.

Driver location is a detail that gets forgotten until commissioning. A remote driver needs a serviceable position — above a ceiling access panel, inside a cove, or in a riser. A driver sealed inside a linear profile with no access is a failure waiting for a date.

Documenting a linear requirement

A complete linear enquiry carries the reflected ceiling plan with the run dimensioned, the section through the slot or cove, the radius and arc for any curve, the required output per meter, the color temperature and CRI, the diffuser type, and the control protocol. Photographs of similar details help where a drawing does not yet exist.
