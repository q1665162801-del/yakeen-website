Why lighting shipments get held in Saudi customs

Lighting is one of the product categories most frequently flagged by Saudi customs. The combination of electrical safety requirements, energy efficiency regulations, and the SABER system means there are multiple points where a shipment can trip up.

Based on our experience with projects across the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia (المملكة العربية السعودية), these are the top reasons lighting shipments get held:

  • Missing or invalid SCoC. The SABER Shipment Conformity Certificate is either missing, expired, or does not match the shipment contents. This is the single most common cause of delays.
  • Unregistered product models. Products in the shipment do not have valid PCoC (Product Conformity Certificate) registration on the SABER platform.
  • Documentation mismatch. The model numbers, quantities, or values on the commercial invoice do not match what is registered in SABER or what appears on the bill of lading.
  • Arabic marking missing. Products arrive without the required Arabic language labeling. SASO inspectors check physical products, not just documents.
  • Energy efficiency non-compliance. Products do not meet the SASO energy efficiency requirements or lack the required energy label registration.
  • Wrong HS code classification. Products are declared under an HS code that does not match the actual product, triggering different (or additional) regulatory checks.
  • Counterfeit or look-alike products. Customs suspects the products are counterfeit or misbranded, triggering a deeper inspection.

The cost of a detention goes far beyond the port storage fees. A two-week delay on a lighting shipment can push back an entire fit-out package, and those liquidated damages typically dwarf the value of the goods themselves.

Required documents for lighting customs clearance

Before your shipment arrives at a Saudi port, make sure all of these are in order. Missing any one of them can result in detention.

Saudi customs clearance document checklist

  • Original bill of lading (sea freight) or airway bill (air freight)
  • Commercial invoice (detailed, with model numbers and unit prices)
  • Packing list (itemized by carton, with weights and dimensions)
  • SABER Shipment Conformity Certificate (SCoC)
  • SABER Product Conformity Certificates (PCoC) for each model
  • SASO IECEE Recognition Certificates
  • Energy efficiency registration confirmation
  • Certificate of origin
  • Insurance certificate (if applicable)
  • Importer commercial registration (Saudi)
  • Customs power of attorney (for broker)

Key detail: the commercial invoice must show individual model numbers, quantities, and unit prices — not just "LED lights" with a lump sum. Customs cross-references the invoice against the SABER registration at the model level.

The customs clearance process step by step

Here is the end-to-end process for clearing lighting shipments through Saudi customs. The same general process applies whether your goods arrive by sea or air.

Step 1: Pre-shipment preparation

Before the goods leave the factory, confirm that every model in the shipment has a valid PCoC on SABER. Apply for the SCoC as soon as you have the final packing list and commercial invoice. Do not wait for the ship to sail.

Step 2: Shipment departure

The goods are loaded and the vessel departs. During transit, confirm that the SCoC has been issued and is electronically linked to the shipment in the SABER system. Our SABER registration guide covers this process in detail.

Step 3: Vessel arrival and manifest filing

The ship arrives at the port (typically Jeddah Islamic Port or King Abdulaziz Port Dammam (الدمام)). The shipping line files the import manifest with Saudi customs. Your broker or freight forwarder starts preparing the customs declaration.

Step 4: Customs declaration

The customs broker files a customs declaration through the Saudi customs system (SADAD / Bayan). The declaration includes HS codes, product descriptions, quantities, values, and country of origin. The SCoC number is referenced in the declaration.

Step 5: Documentary review

Customs reviews the declaration and supporting documents electronically. They check that the SCoC is valid, that the HS codes are correct, and that the declared value appears reasonable. If everything checks out, the shipment may be cleared at this stage without physical inspection.

Step 6: Physical inspection (if selected)

Some percentage of shipments are selected for physical inspection. Customs officers examine the goods to verify they match the documentation — model numbers, quantities, marking, labeling, and general condition. For lighting products, inspectors specifically check for Arabic marking, certification marks, and product safety features.

Step 7: Duty assessment and payment

Once the declaration is accepted (and any inspection passed), customs assesses the import duty and VAT. Standard customs duty on most lighting products is 5%, and VAT is 15% on the CIF value plus duty. Payment is made through the SADAD system.

Step 8: Release and delivery

After payment, customs issues the release order. The broker arranges for container pickup from the port and final delivery to the project site or warehouse.

SABER and the SCoC requirement

The SABER platform (منصة سابر) and the Shipment Conformity Certificate (SCoC) are the gatekeepers for Saudi import clearance. Without a valid SCoC, your shipment will not clear customs — it is that simple.

Here is what you need to understand about how SABER connects to customs:

  • The SCoC is issued through the SABER platform before the shipment arrives
  • Each SCoC is linked to a specific shipment — identified by bill of lading or airway bill number
  • SABER is electronically integrated with Saudi customs, so customs can see the SCoC status in real time
  • If the SCoC is not in the system when the shipment arrives, it goes into detention automatically
  • The SCoC is separate from — and in addition to — the PCoC (product-level certificate)

The most common SCoC mistake: teams assume the SCoC is generated automatically when the PCoC exists. It is not. You must actively apply for an SCoC for each shipment, referencing the specific products, quantities, and shipping documents.

Jeddah Islamic Port vs. King Abdulaziz Port Dammam

Most lighting shipments for Saudi projects enter through either Jeddah or Dammam. Both ports follow the same customs rules, but there are practical differences that affect your project.

Jeddah Islamic Port (جدة)

Jeddah is Saudi Arabia's busiest seaport and the main entry point for the western and central regions — Makkah, Madinah, Jeddah itself, and Riyadh (الرياض) via road connection.

  • Volume: Higher container volume means more congestion during peak periods
  • Inspection rate: Generally higher inspection rates due to larger volumes and more experienced inspection teams
  • Transit to Riyadh: Approximately 800 km by road, typically 1–2 days by truck
  • Best for: Projects in the western and central regions

King Abdulaziz Port Dammam (الدمام)

Dammam is the primary port for the Eastern Province and serves projects in Dammam, Khobar, Dhahran, and the northern regions. It also connects to the GCC road network for cross-border trucking to Bahrain, Kuwait, and Qatar.

  • Volume: Lower volume than Jeddah, generally less congestion
  • Inspection rate: Lower on average, but still thorough for electrical products
  • GCC connectivity: Better for multi-country shipments that continue to Bahrain or Kuwait
  • Best for: Projects in the Eastern Province and northern Saudi Arabia

For Riyadh projects, the choice between Jeddah and Dammam depends on shipping line routes, inland transport costs, and current port congestion. Both are viable — work with your freight forwarder to compare.

Common clearance delays and how to avoid them

These are the delays we see most often, and what to do about them:

1. SCoC not applied in time

What happens: The shipment arrives at the port but no SCoC has been issued. The container goes into detention. You then rush to get the SCoC, which takes 2–5 days assuming all PCoCs are in place.

How to avoid: Apply for the SCoC as soon as the commercial invoice and packing list are final — before the ship sails. Confirm SCoC issuance while the vessel is still at sea.

2. Arabic marking inspection failure

What happens: Customs opens the container and finds products with English-only labeling. The shipment is held until the marking issue is resolved — either by re-labeling at the port (expensive and slow) or by re-exporting.

How to avoid: Verify Arabic marking on samples before production. Include Arabic marking requirements in your PO and quality inspection checklist. Our SASO 2902 guide covers the marking requirements in detail.

3. HS code classification dispute

What happens: Customs classifies the product under a different HS code than what you declared, potentially triggering different duty rates or additional regulatory requirements.

How to avoid: Work with a broker who knows lighting products. Provide detailed product descriptions and technical datasheets so the broker can classify correctly. If in doubt, request an advance ruling from customs.

4. Mixed shipments with uncertified items

What happens: A shipment contains mostly certified fixtures plus a few accessories or control devices that are not registered on SABER. The entire shipment gets held because of the uncertified items.

How to avoid: Check every single line item on your commercial invoice against SABER registration. If something is not registered, either get it registered before shipping or ship it separately under a different clearance route if applicable.

5. Value underdeclaration detected

What happens: Customs flags the declared value as too low compared to their reference database. They may reassess the value and charge additional duty plus penalties.

How to avoid: Declare accurate values. Keep evidence of the transaction (invoices, payment records) in case customs asks for proof. Underdeclaration is not worth the risk.

Post-clearance: market surveillance checks

Customs clearance is not the end of the compliance story. SASO and the Saudi market surveillance authority conduct post-clearance checks on products already in the market.

What this means for your project:

  • Site inspections. Inspectors may visit project sites or warehouses to check that products on the ground match the certification documentation.
  • Product sampling. Products may be sampled and sent for testing to verify they meet the claimed specifications — especially for energy efficiency and safety.
  • Penalties for non-compliance. If post-clearance checks find non-compliant products, the importer or project owner can face fines, product recalls, or forced removal.
  • Blacklisting. Repeat offenders can be added to a watchlist, increasing scrutiny on all their future shipments.

We have heard stories of contractors thinking they can sneak non-compliant product through customs and it will be fine once it is on site. That gamble does not always pay off, and the cost of losing it is high. Better to specify compliant product from the start.

Working with a local customs broker

Do you need a local customs broker? For lighting products entering Saudi Arabia, the answer is almost always yes. Here is why:

  • Local knowledge. A good broker knows the current inspection patterns, which product categories are being flagged, and how to navigate issues when they come up.
  • System access. Brokers have direct access to Saudi customs systems and can file declarations, track status, and resolve issues faster than an overseas importer working remotely.
  • SABER coordination. Brokers who handle electrical products regularly understand the SABER system and can coordinate with certification bodies if there are SCoC issues.
  • Language. Having someone on the ground who speaks Arabic fluently and can talk directly to customs officers is invaluable when things go wrong.
  • Problem resolution. When a shipment is flagged for inspection or detention, a broker who knows the system can often resolve it faster — sometimes in days instead of weeks.

When selecting a broker, look for one with specific experience handling lighting or electrical products. A broker who specializes in food or textiles will not know the ins and outs of SASO 2902 and SABER. Ask for references from other lighting importers.

Quick reference checklist

Use this checklist before each lighting shipment to Saudi Arabia:

Saudi lighting clearance checklist

  • All product models have valid PCoCs on SABER
  • SCoC applied for and confirmed before shipment arrival
  • Commercial invoice matches SABER registration (models, quantities)
  • Products have correct Arabic marking and certification labels
  • Energy efficiency registration confirmed for all models
  • HS codes verified with experienced broker
  • All documents (BL, invoice, packing list, COO) prepared and consistent
  • Customs broker briefed and ready
  • Duty and VAT budgeted (typically 5% + 15% VAT on CIF+ duty)
  • Contingency buffer built into project schedule (minimum 1 week)
